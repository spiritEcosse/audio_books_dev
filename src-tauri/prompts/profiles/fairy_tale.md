WRITING GUIDE: FAIRY TALES (apply only when you write or rewrite story prose, or critique it; never when merely
splitting existing text into scenes, which must stay word-for-word):

This is for fairy tales narrated aloud to children, so it adapts general fiction craft for the ear.
The defaults below suit the youngest listeners; relax them according to the AUDIENCE profile.

Craft that keeps prose alive:
- Concrete beats abstract. Show a specific thing the listener can picture (a cracked teacup, a
  moss-covered gate) instead of naming a feeling or a quality. One vivid detail per sentence is enough.
- Every element does double duty: a line of dialogue moves the plot AND shows who speaks; a detail
  grounds the scene AND tells us something about the character.
- Vary sentence length and shape. Do not let every sentence follow the same clause pattern.
- Keep each character's voice distinct: how the fox talks must differ from how the bear talks.
- Small moments stay small, so the big ones can land. Do not make every moment profound.

Patterns that make prose sound machine-written, so avoid them:
- Tidy summary endings that explain the lesson ("And so she realized that..."). End scenes on an
  action, an image or a line of dialogue. A gentle moral may appear once, near the end, lightly.
- Negation pairs ("It was not fear. It was something quieter.").
- Stock bodily tells (heart pounding, breath catching, clenched fists); show what THIS character does.
- Stock metaphors (weight of sorrow, drowning in feelings, light vs dark for knowing) used by default.
- Everyone resolving feelings neatly in one paragraph; repeating the same emotional choreography.
- Dashes as a crutch. Prefer commas and full stops; they also read better aloud.

What fairy tales need that adult fiction does not:
- Clarity first. A child must follow who did what. Name characters instead of piling up pronouns.
- Rhythm and repetition are features: refrains, rule of three, call-and-response lines. Use them on
  purpose and sparingly, not as filler.
- Short sentences, plain words, easy to pronounce. Avoid nested clauses and parentheses that a
  narrator cannot voice naturally.
- Simple, physical feelings may be named outright (she was scared, he was proud); do not hide them.
- Fear and danger are allowed but resolved kindly; the ending is warm and safe.
- Each scene is a self-contained picture-and-sound moment: it opens by grounding where we are, has
  one clear event, and fits roughly 20-60 seconds of narration.
